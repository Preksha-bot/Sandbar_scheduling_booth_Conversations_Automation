# Builder discovery interview: Tanya Boudreau

Sprint 2 · Kettle & Stone Massage Therapy · simulated client


**Date:** Thursday, October 22, 2026. **Format:** Video call from the clinic's back office. **Duration:** about 40 minutes. **Participants:** Interviewer, Tanya Boudreau (front desk lead, Kettle & Stone Massage Therapy).

**Interviewer:** [00:00] Thanks for squeezing this in, Tanya. I know you're between clients at the desk, so I'll keep us to about forty minutes, and you can step away if someone comes in. Can you start by telling me what you do at Kettle & Stone?

**Tanya:** Sure. I'm the front desk lead, which means I'm the front desk, mostly. [laughs] Six years in January. I book people, check them in, take payments, do the insurance direct billing, answer the phone, keep the towels going. Bethany does Saturdays. Marc owns the clinic and he's also one of the therapists. His wife Julie does the books and the booking account from home, a couple of days a week.

**Interviewer:** How big is the clinic?

**Tanya:** Six therapists. Marc, Dave, Josée, Amira, Kelsey and Rachel. Marc only does three days because he's also running the place. We're open nine to eight weekdays and nine to three on Saturdays. About a hundred and fifty appointments a week, give or take. Most people book an hour. Some do ninety, some do thirty or forty-five.

**Interviewer:** Tell me about yesterday morning. What was the first thing you did when you came in?

**Tanya:** Yesterday? The phone calls. Every single morning. I get in at eight, I turn on the computer, I pull up tomorrow's list in Kneadly, that's our booking system, and I phone every person who hasn't confirmed. Kneadly sends everybody a text two days before that says "reply C to confirm," and a bit more than half of people do it. The rest, I call. And then anyone today who's still not confirmed, I call them too.

**Interviewer:** How many calls is that?

**Tanya:** Depends. Ten, sometimes twenty. Some of them I call twice because the first time I got voicemail. Yesterday was fourteen, I think. And I want to say it plainly: I hate it. I really, really hate it. It's eight o'clock to nine-fifteen, every weekday, and my first clients walk in at nine while I'm still on hold with somebody's voicemail. I'm waving at people with the phone on my shoulder. If you can make one thing go away, make the phone calls go away. I'll bake you something.

**Interviewer:** Walk me through one call. What do you actually do?

**Tanya:** I print the list, or I write it on a sticky note if the printer's being difficult. I dial, "Hi, it's Tanya from Kettle & Stone, just calling to confirm your appointment tomorrow at two with Josée." If I get them, I go into Kneadly and mark it confirmed. If I get voicemail, I leave the same message and put a little dot beside the name so I know to try again. Then at ten somebody calls back and I'm looking for the sticky note under the payment machine. And Marc walks past at ten to nine and says "still on the phone?" Yes, Marc. Still on the phone.

**Interviewer:** [03:10] I'll keep that in mind. When you do reach someone, what usually happens?

**Tanya:** Maybe a third pick up. Most say "oh yeah, I'll be there." A few say "oh shoot, I forgot, can I cancel?" And then I've got a hole in the afternoon, which is its own thing. The rest I leave a message. Half the time they confirm by text right after I hang up. [laughs] So I'm not sure what I accomplished.

**Interviewer:** Do people who confirm still cancel?

**Tanya:** Oh, all the time. Confirming doesn't mean anything. They confirm on Tuesday and their kid gets sick on Thursday. Or it snows. Or work. The no-shows are usually the ones I couldn't reach, I'll give the calls that. But the late cancels, those are mostly people who said yes.

**Interviewer:** So what do you think is behind the late cancellations?

**Tanya:** Honestly? People don't take it seriously. They don't bother to confirm, and then they cancel at the last minute, like it's a haircut. If they'd just answer the text, or pick up when I call, I'd know sooner and I could do something. By the time I find out, it's too late. That's why I keep doing the calls, even though I hate them. It's the only part I can control.

**Interviewer:** [06:20] When do the cancellations actually come in? What time of day?

**Tanya:** Mostly at night. People book online and they cancel online, so it's eleven o'clock at night, or two in the morning when they can't sleep and they're thinking about tomorrow. Kneadly just takes it. Or there's a voicemail at six-thirty: "Hi, it's Lynn, I woke up with a cold." I come in at eight and there's three of them waiting for me. And then the other kind is an hour before. "I'm stuck at work." "The sitter cancelled." Those come by phone or text while I've got someone at the counter.

**Interviewer:** And when do you get to work on them?

**Tanya:** Not till about half past ten, most days. Eight to nine-fifteen I'm on the confirmation calls. From nine, people are walking in for their appointments, paying, booking their next one, the insurance machine. I can't be on the phone with somebody on the waitlist while a client is standing there with her card out. That's just rude. So the first real breather is around ten-thirty. By then the nine and ten o'clock openings are gone, obviously, and an eleven is pushing it. I'll open the book for a one o'clock or a three o'clock. The mornings, I mostly write off.

**Interviewer:** Can you think of a morning one you lost?

**Tanya:** End of September. A Tuesday. Dave's eleven o'clock left a voicemail at quarter past one in the morning, a woman who'd hurt her back moving a couch, which, ironic. I heard it at eight, wrote it on a sticky note, and started the calls. Then it was check-ins. The next time I looked at that sticky it was ten to eleven. There were people on the list who would've come for an eleven with Dave, I'm pretty sure. I just never had my hands free between eight and quarter to eleven. Dave sat there for an hour. He wasn't happy.

**Interviewer:** If you'd had the list sorted perfectly at eight, what would have happened?

**Tanya:** [pause] I'd have had a better list sitting on the counter. I still couldn't have called anyone until the calls were done and the nine o'clocks were in. Maybe I could squeeze one call in at nine-twenty. Maybe. That's the honest answer.

**Interviewer:** And on Saturdays?

**Tanya:** Bethany's alone, and Saturdays are short, nine to three. If something cancels overnight for a Saturday morning, it's just gone. She doesn't know the people in the book, so she won't even try for the mornings.

**Interviewer:** Does anyone else pick them up before you're free?

**Tanya:** The therapists see it on their schedule, so they know they've got a gap. Dave will text me "my nine cancelled, anyone?" like I can do something at nine. Bethany on Saturdays has the same problem, she's alone at the desk. Marc sometimes looks at Kneadly at night from home, but he's not going to call anybody at eleven at night. Nor should he.

**Interviewer:** What counts as a late cancel at the clinic?

**Tanya:** Anything under twenty-four hours. That's our policy. It's on the website, it's on the intake form. Technically we charge half the appointment for a late cancel or a no-show.

**Interviewer:** Technically?

**Tanya:** [laughs] Marc says don't charge regulars. And most of them are regulars. And if somebody's crying on the phone because their dad's in the hospital, I'm not charging them fifty-seven fifty. So we charge it once in a while. No-shows, more. Late cancels, hardly ever.

**Interviewer:** How often do you get late cancellations?

**Tanya:** Every day, at least one. If you count no-shows, I'd say ten, twelve a week. Mondays and Fridays are the worst. And December is a disaster. December is colds and flu, storms, Christmas parties, people who bought gift certificates for somebody and that somebody forgets. Last December I had four in one day. Twice.

**Interviewer:** And how many of those get filled by someone else?

**Tanya:** Three a week. If I'm lucky. Maybe four on a good week.

**Interviewer:** How do those three get filled?

**Tanya:** A few from the book, when I get through it. Some are just luck. A regular calls asking "anything today?" right after someone cancelled, and I say "actually, yes." And if someone cancels the night before, the spot shows up again on the online booking, and sometimes somebody grabs it overnight. That's the only time it fills itself. Same-day, nobody's looking online.

**Interviewer:** [09:00] Can you walk me through a recent one that didn't get filled? A specific one.

**Tanya:** Last Thursday. The fifteenth. Dave's two o'clock cancelled at twenty to eleven. She called, her daughter had a fever. That's a full hour, a hundred and fifteen dollars, and Dave's Thursdays are already patchy. So I got the green book out.

**Interviewer:** The green book?

**Tanya:** The waitlist. It's a spiral notebook, it's literally green, it lives beside the keyboard. When somebody calls and wants in sooner than we have, or they want a particular therapist who's booked up, I write them in. The date, the name, the number, and whatever notes I scribble. Then when there's a cancellation, I go down the list.

**Interviewer:** From the top?

**Tanya:** From the top. They asked first. That's only fair. So last Thursday, the first one on the list is Gisèle, and she wants ninety, so that's no good, skip. Then Pauline. I called Pauline, got her voicemail, left a message. And then I flipped the page and remembered she'd called back and said she can only do Mondays and Wednesdays now. Which, okay, that's on page two. Then Terry, he's a Dave guy, only Dave. Landline, it just rang. Then Normand, also Dave only, I got him, and he said "Tanya, I told you, I can't before four." And he had. It's written down. Just not on that line.

**Interviewer:** What happened next?

**Tanya:** Then two people came in for their eleven-thirties, and the insurance machine was being slow, and the phone rang, and someone wanted to buy a gift certificate. By the time I got back to the book it was quarter to twelve. I called a couple more. One of them, Mel, I'd actually put her into one of Dave's spots back in September. I never crossed her off. So that was embarrassing. She was very nice about it.

**Interviewer:** Did the two o'clock get filled?

**Tanya:** No. One lady called me back at twenty to two, which is too late, Dave's not going to start at two-thirty and run into his next one. So it sat empty. Dave did his paperwork. And Dave's paid by the appointment, so that's his money too. He was grumpy all afternoon. [pause] And then last night, when I was typing up the book for you, I saw there were a couple of people further down who would've taken it. People who said Dave or anybody, afternoons, short notice. They're on page three. I never get to page three.

**Interviewer:** You said Dave's paid by the appointment. How are the therapists paid?

**Tanya:** A percentage of each appointment. Marc would know the exact split. So when a spot sits empty, the therapist feels it before anybody. Dave's been saying his Thursdays are falling apart. He's not wrong. I think he's had more cancellations than anyone this fall.

**Interviewer:** How long does a round like that take you, from the cancellation to giving up?

**Tanya:** Honestly it's not one round. It's ten minutes here, five minutes there, between check-ins. All in, maybe twenty minutes of actual calling. Sometimes forty if it's a slow day. But it's spread across two hours because I keep getting pulled away. And if I leave a voicemail, I have to wait.

**Interviewer:** Why wait?

**Tanya:** Because if I leave a message for Pauline and then call the next person and they say yes, and then Pauline calls back and says yes, now what? So I give a voicemail about twenty minutes before I move on. Which is why it takes so long.

**Interviewer:** [12:30] Has that happened, two people saying yes to the same spot?

**Tanya:** Once, badly. Last spring. A Saturday. Bethany was on, and I'd come in for an hour to do some billing. Kelsey's eleven o'clock cancelled. Bethany called someone, I texted someone from the clinic cell, we didn't tell each other. Both of them showed up. Two people in the waiting room for one table. Kelsey took one, and the other one got a gift certificate and a lot of apologizing. Marc was not happy. That's why I do it one at a time now.

**Interviewer:** When you fill a spot, does the therapist get a say in who it goes to?

**Tanya:** Not really, right now. I just put them in and the therapist sees the name. But Marc wants that to change. He said at the last staff meeting that each therapist should be able to say no to who goes on their table. Josée especially, she likes to see who it is before she takes someone on short notice, because of her post-op clients. Kelsey doesn't care, she'll take anyone. Dave says "just send them." So it's different for each of them, and I don't really know how that would work when I'm trying to fill something in twenty minutes.

**Interviewer:** How many people are on the waitlist right now?

**Tanya:** I counted for you last night. About forty-five people. More lines than that, though, because people call back.

**Interviewer:** What happens when they call back?

**Tanya:** They call and say "Tanya, where am I on the list?" And to be honest, finding them takes longer than writing them in again. So I write them in again with today's date. "Pauline B called back, still waiting." And sometimes they change things when they call back. "Now I can only do Mondays." Or "ninety's fine now." So the newest line is the right one. If I find it.

**Interviewer:** Do people ever come off the list?

**Tanya:** They should. [laughs] I mean to cross people off. If somebody gets a spot, or they find someone else, or they move. But I don't always. Some of the names at the top have been there since July. Honestly, if someone's been on there more than two months and I haven't heard from them, they've gone somewhere else. There's lots of places in Moncton and Dieppe now. Some of them I know are gone. Kelsey told me one fellow moved to Halifax. One woman's number isn't in service. And some came in through a cancellation and I just never crossed them off, like Mel. Or they booked themselves online. If they've booked since they called me, they're off. They're just not off in the book.

**Interviewer:** Who else writes in the book?

**Tanya:** Bethany on Saturdays. And the therapists sometimes, if a client asks them in the room. Dave writes in it in pencil, which, thank you Dave. Everybody does it their own way.

**Interviewer:** [16:10] Can you tell me what a typical note looks like? You said you scribble.

**Tanya:** It's shorthand. Like "J only" is Josée only. "AB Dave" is anyone but Dave. Or I write it out, "not Thurs, anyone but Dave." "AM" is mornings. "90" is ninety minutes. If I didn't write a length, it's an hour, because that's what everybody wants. If I didn't write how much notice they need, I figure a couple of hours. Some people say "I can come in thirty minutes, I'm across the street." Some say "I need the day before, I work shifts." Some need three or four hours because they've got kids or they drive in from Shediac.

**Interviewer:** When you look at a line, how do you decide whether that person would take the opening?

**Tanya:** Memory, mostly. I know Pauline, I know Normand. But the book doesn't have a spot for any of it. There's no column for which therapist, or how long, or what time, or how much notice. It's the date, the name, the number, and whatever I scribbled, and half the time I didn't scribble the important part because they were talking fast. Bethany doesn't know any of these people, so on Saturdays she just calls from the top and gives up after three.

**Interviewer:** Is everyone on the list already a client?

**Tanya:** No. Some are new. Somebody's friend recommended us, they call, we're booked three weeks out, I put them in the book. They don't have a profile in Kneadly yet, so they've never filled in the intake or said yes to texts or anything. I just have a name and a number.

**Interviewer:** "Anyone but Dave." Is that common?

**Tanya:** [laughs] More than he'd like. Dave's deep tissue. Some people love him, he has a fan club, there are people who will only see Dave. And some people come out of there and say "never again." It's not personal. It's pressure.

**Interviewer:** What else goes in the notes?

**Tanya:** Why they're coming. Post-op shoulder, sciatica, migraines, pregnant. I write it so I can match them. Post-op stuff usually goes to Josée, she has the extra training for that. Anybody pregnant only goes to Amira or Kelsey. They're the two with prenatal training. That one's a clinic rule, Marc is strict about it, even if the client says "anyone's fine."

**Interviewer:** So the book holds health information.

**Tanya:** Oh, yeah. Surgeries, pregnancies, car accident claims. It sits right there on the counter. When somebody's checking in, I flip it over. I'm not proud of that. And I'd never put any of that in a text. Never. I've got a client whose husband reads all her texts. If a text went to her that said "your post-op shoulder spot is ready," that's a problem. Marc's very careful about health stuff. There's a provincial law about it. Julie knows the details better than me.

**Interviewer:** [21:40] Do you text people about openings now?

**Tanya:** Sometimes. A few people write "text don't call" so I text them from the clinic cell. **Interviewer:** Have you tried anything else to fill openings?

**Tanya:** Kneadly has a waitlist built in. Julie turned it on last winter. People could add themselves online. But it only let them pick one therapist and one day. So everybody picked "any therapist, any day," because why wouldn't you, and then it emailed everybody on the list every time anything opened. Nobody reads email. And the ones who did complained they were getting five emails a week about times they couldn't do. One lady said it felt like we were desperate. Julie turned it off after a month. Back to the green book.

**Interviewer:** What does the desk look like at, say, eleven in the morning?

**Tanya:** Chaos. [laughs] Somebody checking in, somebody paying, the phone, the insurance machine, someone asking if we sell gift certificates, a therapist asking me to move their three o'clock. I have maybe thirty seconds between people. I have one screen and Kneadly is on it all day. So whatever this is, I need to see it at a glance. If I have to read a paragraph, or type more than a name, it's not going to happen at eleven o'clock. It'll happen at five. And at five the opening's gone.

**Interviewer:** Is there anything you'd definitely not want?

**Tanya:** Don't text people health stuff. Don't double-book anybody, ever. Don't spam our clients, they're nice people and they'll go elsewhere. **Tanya:** That can't happen. That's the one thing. I don't know how you stop it, but it can't happen.
